# The eight sites vs. our brainstorming (2026-10-29)

Each section follows `brainstorming.md`. "Supports" = at least one reviewed site does something close to what we discussed. "Challenges" = they do it differently or suggest a risk. "No precedent" = none of the eight does it, so we'd be on our own.
Evidence limits: desktop only, static screenshots (no animation or hover seen), mostly home and service pages, a link crawl without JavaScript.

## 1. Goals and conversion (§1)

**Contact + subscribe as the two goals**
- *Supports:* Allie Miller and Kavita Ganesan both put the two actions side by side (Allie: Contact button + free email course banner; Kavita: "Join my newsletter" + "Get expert advice" in the hero).
- *Challenges:* the strongest converter, Datalumina, uses **one** action ("Submit Project Request") everywhere. Rachel Woods uses email capture only. A two-goal page needs a clear primary.

**Form vs. email**
- Nobody relies on a bare email link as the main route. Datalumina uses an external Typeform; Kavita and Drew Breunig use a form; Allie routes to a request page (speaking via an agency). Several also show an address in the footer (Allie, Datalumina, Kavita). Matt Webb's email is the exception, and it's hidden.
- None of the sites say anything about spam, so this doesn't settle your question. A form plus a visible footer address is the common combination.

**Missing from the brainstorm: a lead magnet.** The two biggest email lists (Allie's 5-day course, Rachel's playbook template) and Datalumina Academy's free proposal framework all give something away. Subscribing "for updates" is the weaker pattern (Drew, Jason Liu). Worth deciding what John gives for an email.

## 2. The three threads and their pain points (§2)

**Framing by fear**
- *Supports:* Datalumina's "You don't have to... / Instead you could..." box (meetings, vague proposals, strategy with nothing built) and its "95% of GenAI projects fail" line are the clearest examples of pain-then-promise. Kavita also frames against "guesswork, jargon, and tool-chasing."
- *Challenges:* those fears are **already felt** by the reader (agencies are slow, AI projects fail). The "web goes dark" fear is **new**, so a visitor may not feel it yet. None of the eight sells a problem the audience hasn't met. Expect to need a concrete scene that makes the fear tangible.

**Named vocabulary**
- *Supports:* Rachel Woods ("playbooks," CRAFT, "AI Operator"), Drew Breunig ("Prompt Debt," "Context Inertia"). Memorable names make a method feel like a thing. You have a few ("agentic web," "dark web," "virtuous cycle," "brain transfer"); the visitor-facing phrasing is still open.

**Unequal strength among threads**
- Every single-offer site (Datalumina, Rachel) wins clarity by saying one thing. The only multi-thread sites are Kavita's (a big catalog) and Allie's (speaking, advising, courses). See §3.

## 3. Site structure (§3)

**Three storylines, each with its own URL, same template**
- *Supports, strongly:* Kavita's company site is nearly this: about 20 pages, each a service, industry, or company-size page of 1,200-3,000 words ending in one contact action, cross-linked to each other. Datalumina Academy's program pages (Accelerator, Launchpad, Freelance) also share a template (curriculum, pricing, FAQ, reviews, enroll).
- *Challenges:* Kavita gets there with scale and SEO volume. Three pages with equal weight on one homepage, at your size, is different. This tension is real, but we've already settled on equal treatment, so it's a design problem to solve, not a reason to reverse.

**Fixed shell**
- *Supports:* Kavita's company site and Datalumina Academy both keep a constant header and footer across all pages.

**One umbrella sentence**
- *Supports:* every site that converts has one. Datalumina: "Blazing fast builds for AI-forward companies." Kavita: "Strategic AI Integration to Corporations and the Boardroom." Rachel: "The future of work runs on AI playbooks." Each names a thing delivered and a type of customer.
- *Closest to your situation:* Matt Webb's three overlapping circles (AI, Embodiment, Multiplayer) are the only visible attempt to unify several threads. They carry no explanation, so he shows the idea without making it understood.

**Separate personal hub and sales site**
- Dave Ebbelaar and Kavita both split a personal site from a company site. Kavita's split costs clarity (three domains, a "Get Expert Advice" button on the personal site that lands on the company home page). Worth avoiding.

## 4. The "pick your problem" homepage (§4)

- **No precedent.** None of the eight asks the visitor a question and re-shapes the page around the answer. Closest cousins: Eleanor Berger's stacked course cards, Datalumina Academy's "Find your program" menu item, and Kavita's menu of problem-specific pages. All are menus, not conversations.
- *Implication:* the idea is distinctive, and there's no evidence here that it converts. We'd be testing it ourselves. The safest framing is to keep every answer a real link to a real page (as in §3), so the idea fails soft.
- **Escape route / switching:** no site lets you switch storylines in place. The sites with many paths use a standard menu.
- **Where visitors get stranded** (relevant to your escape-route idea): Matt Webb's contact is buried, Allie's advising has no page, and Datalumina's sales page has only one exit.

## 5. Style and design (§5)

- **Scrollytelling:** none of the eight appears to use it. The nearest are Allie's full-bleed hero and Datalumina's glowing hero. This reviewed the wrong class of site for scroll style; Andrei Moise's site and twotimespi.dev are better references (still unopened).
- **Avoiding the generic AI look:** Datalumina is the generic dark/glowing look. The distinctive ones are Eleanor Berger's yellow neo-brutalism, Matt Webb's cyan diagram, and Rachel Woods' pastel mascot. Plain sites (Drew, Jason) get by on writing alone.
- **Top-down "worldview" hero** (your new idea): only Matt Webb has a diagram hero, and it's decorative. An interactive map of John's interests with explanations would be new.
- **Clarity first:** the sites that state audience and offer in the first screen (Datalumina, Kavita) are the clearest. Those that open with identity and mood (Matt Webb, Drew) leave visitors to infer.

## 6. Branding (§6)

- Not compared in depth. Observation only: the memorable brands each pair a simple visual with a plain phrase (a yellow block, a mascot, a cyan diagram). None uses a story or myth behind its name the way "keeper of the bear" could.

## 7. Content and proof (§7)

**Testimonials with role (your planned approach)**
- *Supports:* Datalumina names person, role, and company on every quote, and it reads as more credible than Rachel Woods' first-name-and-initial reviews.
- **Gap in your quotes:** Datalumina's strongest proof has **numbers** (38% support-cost savings, 43% cost reduction, under 200 ms). The three Arcturus quotes (Perch, CETA, Peer Supply) are qualitative. If any client can supply a figure ("research time cut from X to Y"), it would strengthen them. This matches the open question in §11.

**Bio and credentials**
- *Challenges:* Allie's and Kavita's About sections are walls of credentials. Their credibility comes from named employers and clients, which skim well as logos. Your bio is strong (Copilot, two books) and should be a short stack, not a paragraph wall.
- Kavita builds the whole site around one book. Yours are older; use them as supporting proof.

**Speaking**
- *Supports:* Matt Webb's speaking page (about 35 items by year, with video links and an "Internal" tag for company talks) and Allie's named audiences and quotes show speaking history as proof. Your list (Hugo, Hamel, Doug, O'Reilly, and the 30K-view podcast) fits that pattern.

**Blog as per-path evidence**
- *Supports:* Jason Liu's series hubs (a context-engineering index linked from 11 pages) and "Start Here" list, Drew's tag pages, and Kavita's blog posts linked from service pages all do this.

**Community and events** (central to your strategy)
- Almost no site foregrounds community. The nearest analog is Eleanor Berger's recurring live "Sunday School: Drop In, Vibe On," listed as one of five offerings. Rachel Woods has a membership. **Nobody presents a regular free event as the front door.** You'll need to design this yourself.

## 8. Ways to work together (§8)

- *Supports:* Kavita's "Four ways to work together" (programs, keynote, coaching, training) is close to your four formats and is two sentences each with one button. Datalumina packages one thing with a timeline and capacity ("live in 2 weeks," "2 new clients per month").
- *Challenges:* the most common weakness is vague advising (Allie). Your older note already has a concrete four-meeting assessment, which is more specific than anything reviewed.
- **Pricing:** only courses show prices (Rachel's $3,000; Datalumina Academy has a pricing section). No consulting site shows rates. Whether to show your assessment price is a real decision, and the sites suggest most don't.
- **Easy first step:** Datalumina's "what you'll get" list under its form (clear feedback, realistic scope and timeline, fast-track to the sprint) is the best example of an expected-outcome first step.
- **Fit signals:** Kavita does this with industry and company-size pages; Datalumina names its customer ("AI-forward companies").

## 9. Agent-readiness (§9)

- *Supports:* Drew Breunig (llms.txt with summaries and dates, every post also served as Markdown, an Atom feed, JSON-LD) and Dave Ebbelaar's personal site (llms.txt linked in the footer) show it done in practice. Drew's llms.txt is a good template for per-storyline entries.
- *No precedent:* none of the eight has `.well-known` agent skills or any WebMCP tools. An agent-capable site would be unique in this set, which suits your positioning, and also means there are no models to copy.
- Matt Webb's and Rachel's robots.txt use Cloudflare's "content signals" preamble (a way to state what AI may do with content), which is worth a look.

## 10. Things in your backlog the sites confirm or complicate

- **Floating subscribe / exit-intent:** none of the eight uses a floating prompt, though Allie uses a top banner and several repeat a subscribe block at the bottom.
- **Chatbot or Calendly:** none shows a chatbot. Datalumina's form-first, human-review model ("engineers will review your idea... confirm if it's a fit") is the nearest.
- **Case studies:** Datalumina Academy has three on its Freelance page; Matt Webb's project pages read as short case stories. The format works.

## 11. New items this comparison suggests adding

- Decide a **lead magnet**.
- Decide whether to show **price** on the assessment.
- Look for **one number per testimonial** from past clients.
- Pick **one primary action** per page, with the other as secondary.
- Decide how **community events** appear on the homepage (no model exists).
- Look at **true scrollytelling references** rather than consultant sites.
- Make each storyline end in **one consistent action**, like Kavita's service pages.
- Draft the **umbrella sentence** using the "outcome + customer" pattern.
